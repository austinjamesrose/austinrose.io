## Why Pokemon?

As I was experimenting with Claude Code over the holiday, I wanted to practice workforce analytics, the kind of analysis HR teams use to understand talent pipelines, identify skill gaps, and spot high performers. The problem? Real workforce data is sensitive. You can't publish an analysis of your company's performance reviews.

I needed a proxy dataset. Something with enough complexity to be interesting, enough structure to apply real frameworks, and enough public familiarity that a reader could follow along.

Pokemon, a hobby I have kept up for years, fit.

Every Pokemon has stats (skills), types (departments), abilities (behavioral traits), evolution stages (career levels), and competitive usage data (performance ratings). Twenty-five years of design decisions sit behind more than a thousand creatures. It amounts to a fictional HR database.

I planned a methodology exercise and came away with findings about game design patterns, plus a few lessons about analytics pitfalls that apply well beyond Pokemon.

---

## The Setup

I mapped Pokemon concepts to workforce equivalents:

- **Base Stats** → Core Competencies
- **Type** → Department/Function
- **Evolution Stage** → Career Level
- **Competitive Usage** → Performance Rating
- **Generation** → Tenure Cohort

With 1,025 Pokemon spanning 9 generations, I had a rich dataset to cluster, analyze, and stress-test. Five findings stood out.

---

## 5 Things I Found

### 1. Clustering Found Five Roles I Never Defined

I ran a clustering algorithm on Pokemon stats without telling it anything about types, names, or game lore. It found 5 distinct groups:

- **Defensive Anchors** (10%): High defense, low speed. The compliance and QA of the Pokemon world.
- **Emerging Talent** (36%): Low stats, early evolutions. Your junior associates.
- **Swift Strikers** (21%): Fast and balanced. Sales, agile teams.
- **Technical Specialists** (15%): High special attack, deep expertise. Senior individual contributors.
- **Workhorse Executors** (18%): High HP and attack. Operations leads.

The algorithm had no idea what a "tank" or "sweeper" meant in Pokemon terms. It found them from the numbers alone.

![Five radar charts displaying stat signatures for each workforce archetype. Defensive Anchors show a pronounced Defense spike. Emerging Talent has uniformly low stats across all dimensions. Swift Strikers peak in Speed with balanced offense. Technical Specialists spike in Special Attack. Workhorse Executors show high HP and Attack.](/images/pokemon/synthesis_archetypes_radar.png)

**The HR insight**: Most organizations define job families top-down. Leadership decides there should be "analysts" and "managers" and "specialists," then slots people into those boxes. Clustering your own workforce data reverses that order and shows you which roles already exist.

Cluster on competency assessments, skills inventories, or project contribution patterns. The groupings you find often cut across the org chart. Your "Senior Analysts" might split into two profiles, one technical and one client-facing, sharing a title and little else.

The same analysis shows when your job architecture fights reality. If your clusters do not map to your levels, your leveling system measures the wrong things. Six stats produced five clean archetypes here, and your competency data carries more signal than six stats.

---

### 2. Game Freak Designs to Stereotypes

I expected to find talent gaps: type and role combinations that should exist but do not, like Rock-type speedsters or Electric-type tanks.

The first pass found 11 apparent gaps.

Then I applied a statistical correction for testing so many combinations at once. When you check 90 different cells, some look significant by chance alone. After that correction, **zero gaps survived**.

Five patterns survived, all of them places where Game Freak *over*-indexes:

- Rock types skew defensive
- Steel types skew defensive
- Electric types skew fast
- Psychic types skew toward special attack
- Fairy types skew toward special attack

Game Freak builds to archetype. Rock means slow and sturdy, Electric means fast, and those fantasies drive the stat lines.

![Heatmap matrix of 18 Pokemon types versus 5 workforce roles. Cells are colored by standardized residuals from red (over-indexed) to blue (under-indexed). Gold borders highlight statistically robust patterns: Rock and Steel types cluster heavily in Defensive Anchor, Electric in Swift Striker, and Psychic and Fairy in Technical Specialist.](/images/pokemon/synthesis_type_role_heatmap.png)

**The HR insight**: Organizations do the same thing without noticing. Finance hires analytical introverts. Sales hires charismatic extroverts. These patterns feel natural because they match our mental models of what those roles should be.

Mental models are not strategy. Cross-tabulate department against competency profile in your own workforce and you will find the same over-indexing. Your sales team might be 80% one archetype when performance would improve with a 60/40 mix. Your engineering org might lack the communication-heavy profiles that carry cross-functional work.

Some clustering is useful, so the goal is visibility rather than elimination. Run the analysis to see where you are over-indexed, then ask whether that reflects strategy or pattern-matching. Game Freak designed Rock types to be slow because the fantasy demanded it. Most hiring managers do not have that excuse.

### 3. Speed Correlates with Flexibility

I built a versatility score combining move pool diversity, stat flexibility, and ability options to find which Pokemon can do the most things.

Swift Strikers, the fast ones, came out on top. They have the broadest skill sets and dominate the list of "T-shaped talent" (specialists who can also flex into other roles).

![Two-panel figure. Left panel: Box plot of Versatility Index by role showing Swift Strikers with the highest median and mean scores. Right panel: Bar chart of T-shaped leaders by role, with Swift Strikers contributing 28 of 50 leaders, followed by Workhorse Executors at 15, Technical Specialists at 6, Defensive Anchors at 1, and Emerging Talent excluded from analysis.](/images/pokemon/synthesis_versatility_role.png)

**The HR insight**: Speed-focused creatures carry broader move pools because Game Freak built them to adapt. The same pattern shows up in the roles that develop versatile employees.

Roles that demand constant context-switching, including product management, consulting, and operations, attract or develop people with broader skill sets, because the job requires it. Deep specialist roles like your Technical Specialists and Defensive Anchors build depth at the cost of breadth. Both produce strong people along different development paths.

If you design rotation programs or identify future leaders, trace where your most versatile employees came from. I would bet on the roles that forced them to move fast and touch everything.

### 4. High Performers Have Depth AND Breadth

Using competitive usage data from Smogon (where competitive players track what performs), I built a talent grid mapping performance against flexibility.

The top performers combined deep expertise in their role with enough versatility to adapt.

Dragonite, Zapdos, Clefable, and Tyranitar all score high on role fit and on cross-functional capability.

![Scatter plot of 1,025 Pokemon on a 9-box grid with Performance Score on the y-axis and Flexibility Score on the x-axis. Points are colored by workforce role. Quadrant labels identify Star Performers (upper-right, high on both), Consistent Performers (upper-left, high performance but lower flexibility), Hidden Gems (lower-right, high flexibility but underutilized), and Under Performers (lower-left). Star Performers cluster in the upper-right corner with a mix of all archetypes.](/images/pokemon/synthesis_nine_box.png)

**The HR insight**: The T-shaped professional is a well-worn framework. Organizations measure depth through technical assessments, certifications, and years of experience, or breadth through project counts and cross-functional exposure, and rarely both together.

The Pokemon data made this concrete. When I plotted performance against flexibility, the star performers sat in the quadrant that required both: high role fit, meaning they excel at their specific job, plus high versatility, meaning they adapt when conditions change. Dragonite carries power and flexibility together, a rare combination.

Talent reviews need two questions instead of one: how good is this person in the current role, and how well would they adapt if the role changed? A specialist who cannot flex becomes a single point of failure, and a generalist who has mastered nothing becomes a permanent floater. The people who clear both bars are your bench strength.

Build your 9-box grids to capture this. Performance on one axis, flexibility on the other. Your high-potential population might look different than you expected.

### 5. My Analysis Was Wrong (And How I Fixed It)

My first network analysis identified Magikarp as the most "central" Pokemon, the one that bridges the most connections across the game world. The weakest, most mocked Pokemon as the most important connector made for a good story.

The finding did not hold.

Magikarp appeared central because it shows up everywhere. It's in almost 200 locations, designed as the most common Pokemon in the games. My centrality metric measured how often a Pokemon appears.

Once I controlled for location count, Audino took the top spot. It appears in a moderate number of places and bridges two otherwise disconnected game regions.

![Four-panel figure showing the Magikarp centrality correction. Top-left: Scatter plot revealing location count dominates raw betweenness centrality (ρ = 0.82), with Magikarp as a high outlier due to appearing in 198 locations. Top-right: Bar chart of residual betweenness showing Audino as the most unexpectedly central Pokemon after controlling for location count. Bottom-left: Most efficient bridges ranked by centrality per location. Bottom-right: Histogram of cross-regional experience showing 310 Pokemon appear in 3 or more game regions.](/images/pokemon/network_residual_centrality.png)

**The HR insight**: This mistake is the easiest one to make in people analytics. Network analysis has become aspirational in the field, mapping who collaborates with whom, who's central to information flow, who bridges different teams.

Raw network metrics invite misreading. My centrality score measured how often something showed up. In your organization, the employee with the highest betweenness centrality might be your longest-tenured person, the one who has touched the most projects, or someone copied on a lot of email threads. The metric cannot tell those apart.

Before you identify key connectors or flight risks based on network position, ask what's confounding the measurement. Tenure? Role scope? Meeting culture? I residualized my centrality scores against location count to find the connectors worth naming. You might need to control for tenure, level, or function to find yours.

## Wrapping Up

The transfer surprised me more than any single finding did.

Workforce analytics concepts like competency clustering, talent grids, and network analysis weren't designed for fictional creatures with six stats and elemental types. But they worked. They surfaced patterns in 25 years of game design that I haven't seen articulated elsewhere: the way Game Freak builds to archetype instead of filling gaps, the connection between speed-focused design and versatility, the T-shaped profile of competitively successful Pokemon.

The same dynamics play out in real organizations. Companies hire to stereotype. Fast-paced roles develop flexible employees. High performers combine depth with adaptability. Network metrics get confounded by tenure. The Pokemon data let me show them in a way anyone can explore and verify.

A year ago this project would have stayed on my someday list, because the data pipeline, the clustering, and the visualizations added up to more than I could take on. The tools have changed since then, and so has what one person can build while learning.

If you work in people analytics, I hope something here sparked an idea. If you came for the Pokemon, I hope the games look a little different now.
